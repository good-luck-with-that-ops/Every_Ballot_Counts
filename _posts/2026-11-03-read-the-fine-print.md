I've spent fifteen years reading contracts for a living, and the first thing you learn is that the promise is never the interesting part. The interesting part is what the drafter carefully left out.

There's an old line in politics, usually credited to Reagan: if you're explaining, you're losing. (Yes, I'm quoting Reagan. When I say every ballot counts, I mean the big tent.)

So here's a lawyer's read of what federal officials have told us to expect around this election. I'm more interested in what they're not telling us, and in what they're not *not* doing. Are they trying to avoid [the treatment DOJ got last night](/blog/2026/11/02/two-losses-one-state/) by never saying the loud part out loud? Sticking to the exact words the Attorney General said they could? Baiting nerdy pundits like me for sport?

All jokes aside, we have no idea whether any of these gaps are intentional, and it wouldn't be the first time this administration said something for shock value. I'm just saying: it's an awful lot of explaining.

## Four questions to ask of any promise

1. **Which agency** does it name, and which does it leave out?
2. **Which place:** the polling place, or also the drop box, election office and counting center?
3. **Which time:** Election Day, or the weeks of counting and certification through Jan. 3?
4. **"Will not," or only "no plans"?**

## The promises

**"There will be no ICE presence at polling locations."** (DHS, Feb. 2026) <span class="m">R</span>
It names ICE. Customs and Border Protection and Border Patrol are not ICE, and at the Florida early-voting sites in October the agents people saw were Border Patrol.

**"ICE is not planning operations targeting polling locations."** (DHS, July and Sept.) <span class="m">R</span>
"Planning" is about today. "Operations" leaves out a single arrest or warrant. "Targeting" leaves out a polling place reached incidentally. "Polling locations" leaves out drop boxes, election offices and counting centers.

**DHS could be at polls if there's "a threat to that polling place or we're serving a warrant on someone."** (Secretary Mullin, Sept. 1) <span class="m">R</span>
Every exception is decided by DHS. An administrative immigration warrant is signed by an officer, not a judge.

**"The Joint Force has no plans to send Federal military personnel or Federalized members of the National Guard to polling places."** (Gen. Caine, Aug. 31) <span class="m">D</span>
"No plans" isn't "will not." "Federal" and "Federalized" leave out the Guard under a governor's control. "Polling places" leaves out counting sites after the polls close.

**The FBI won't seize ballots or machines "ahead of the midterms."** (Director Patel, Oct.) <span class="m">R</span>
"Ahead of" ends tomorrow. Counting runs for weeks after, and DOJ has already told all 50 states to preserve ballots for 22 months.

**DOJ's election monitors "will not be acting as 'law enforcement personnel' ... as commonly understood."** (DOJ court filing, Oct. 28) <span class="m">D</span>
"While monitoring" describes a role, not who the person is, and "as commonly understood" is a hedge inside a hedge. The "no agents" language covers the FBI and ICE. According to the NAACP, DEA, ATF and Bureau of Prisons staff are named without it.

> **Update, Nov. 3:** A judge closed this gap. On Nov. 2, Judge Reggie Walton ordered DOJ to make sure its monitors "are indeed administrative or clerical personnel and not law enforcement officials," with no uniforms or insignia, no firearms and no "power to effectuate arrests," and that they "not talk with any voter inside the polling place" ([order](https://www.courtlistener.com/?q=%22NAACP%22+%2226-cv-03626%22)). <span class="m">D</span> He also said his view "would differ" if DOJ were sending officers with arrest power. A promise with a hedge became a court order without one.

**"We have no plans to question or investigate people we believe to be U.S. citizens."** (DHS, in court, Oct. 21) <span class="m">D</span>
It turns on what DHS believes. DHS's own California list treated thousands of people as "likely" noncitizens, and outside researchers found citizens on it.

**The grant conditions that required running every voter and poll worker through SAVE were rescinded, but "will immediately become effective" if the court order is vacated.** (FEMA, Oct. 7) <span class="m">D</span>
They're suspended, not withdrawn.

**And the law itself.** Federal law bars bringing "troops or armed men" to a place where an election is held "unless such force be necessary to repel armed enemies of the United States" (18 U.S.C. 592). <span class="m">D</span> My read: the exception turns on a foreign enemy, and this year the government has described election threats as foreign attacks over and over. <span class="m">I</span>

## Not every gap is a plan

Lawyers hedge by habit. Some of these gaps are probably just careful drafting. But when promise after promise leaves out the same things (other agencies, the counting center, the weeks after Election Day), that's where I'd watch.

The good news so far is that the courts have been the ones closing gaps. As of last night, five courts, including the Supreme Court, have ruled against DOJ's push to hold or set aside ballots before the count. Overnight the list got longer. Judge Chutkan ordered DHS and DOJ to stop sending lists of alleged noncitizen voters to state and local election officials ([opinion](https://storage.courtlistener.com/recap/gov.uscourts.dcd.297656/gov.uscourts.dcd.297656.31.0.pdf)). In her words, the government "collected millions of voting records—including those of U.S. citizens," and no Privacy Act notice covers sharing them. <span class="m">D</span> The full Ninth Circuit also threw out a panel's late-night pause of Judge Thompson's order protecting California election workers. <span class="m">D</span> Court orders have edges too, though: the nine-state ban on DHS election field operations ends when polls close tonight.
