A shared phrase can have innocent explanations (shared industry vocabulary, a common template, text copied from an earlier public source); each entry notes the competing explanations.

## Phrase families we watch

The trail is how this project finds connections: the same words turning up in an order, then a contract, then a court filing. Every new government document gets checked against it. The ones marked ★ sound like routine paperwork but change who can do what.

- **Who counts as a voter:** "undiluted," "diluting the votes of citizens," "lawful voter"; "likely noncitizen"; "may have subsequently naturalized"; "high confidence"; ★"minor data discrepancies"; "voter eligibility"; ★"verification platforms"; "are or were"; "aliens or USCs"; "presumptively valid"; "purporting." Full chain: [#2 below](#who-counts-as-a-voter).
- **Who counts as law enforcement:** ★"law enforcement organization" and "designated law-enforcement entity"; ★"cross-designated"; ★"Special Attorney"; "coordinate with federal immigration authorities"; "investigative or law enforcement function"; ★"as commonly understood"; "administrative or clerical personnel"; ★"law enforcement exception." Full chain: [#1 below](#law-enforcement); ★"law enforcement sensitive" (row "The stamp").
- **What counts as an emergency or attack:** "foreign malign influence"; "significant incident"; ★"public confidence" (full chain: [#3 below](#public-confidence)); "every authorized asset," "whole of government," "no-fail mission"; ★"Executive direction"; "armed enemies"; "election infrastructure."
- **What counts as fraud or crime:** "integrity"; "voter fraud organization"; "organized campaigns"; ★"aiders and abettors"; ★"knowingly"; "criminal aliens." Full chain: [#4 below](#fraud-or-crime).
- **Access to everything (authority):** "full and prompt access," "unfettered access," "any data asset, information, or record," ★"through subpoena where necessary," "every authorized asset," "whole of government," ★"Executive direction," "presidential mandate." Full chain: [#5 below](#access-to-everything).
- **One record per person (data):** "a single, trusted source," "one consolidated record," "person-centric," "eliminate data silos," "seamless," ★"enrich," "compiled list of individual profiles," "continuous monitoring." Full chain: [#6 below](#one-record-per-person).
- **Limits written in, then left open:** for data programs, see the "Limit" rows in #5 and #6; for polling-place promises ("solely on the basis," "no plans," "will not"), see the promises table below and the post [Read the fine print](/blog/2026/11/03/read-the-fine-print/).

## #1 · "Law enforcement": the label that opens and closes doors {#law-enforcement}

"Law enforcement" carve-outs exist for good reasons: police need records, and investigators need room to work. That's what makes the label useful. Say it out loud and a door opens. Disclaim it and a limit goes away. This year the label has been used three ways:

- **Added:** give a person, office or contract the label, and powers and data follow.
- **Disclaimed:** say "not law enforcement" where the label would trigger limits.
- **Exception:** invoke the label to get past a rule.

Rows are in date order. Each one quotes the source's exact words. The first column is the short label (these will become filters on the timeline).

And the next morning DOJ told the court there is "no prohibition" on sworn officers as monitors at all: the line it drew was its choice, and the court's order is what makes it stick. Watch for a new privacy notice whose "routine use" is phrased around "law enforcement purposes": that's the paperwork fix Chutkan's ruling invites.

## #2 · "Who counts as a voter": the words around the lists {#who-counts-as-a-voter}

Every list this year starts from the same idea: noncitizens are voting, and their votes "dilute" citizens' votes. Read the papers in order and you can watch the words change. The theory talks about citizens. The lists talk about confidence scores. And the fine print, in the government's own documents, keeps admitting that citizens are on the lists.

- **Framed:** the theory. Whose votes count, and what threatens them.
- **Flagged:** the label a person gets on a list.
- **Hedged:** the government's own fine print admitting the list catches citizens.
- **Checked:** a court or a government lawyer testing the list.

Rows are in date order. Each one quotes the source's exact words. The first column is the short label.

**What it shows (our read, I):** the theory is about protecting citizens' votes. The lists built under it are scored by "confidence," not checked against any citizenship record, because, as one court put it, no such database exists. And at every step the government's own papers hedge: "may include those of U.S. born citizens," "aliens or USCs," "are or were," "may have subsequently naturalized." By November the courts were calling the lists data "purporting" to show noncitizens.

## #3 · "Public confidence": the feeling that counts as harm {#public-confidence}

Everybody wants voters to trust elections. That's what makes "public confidence" a handy phrase. In two federal authorities, damage to public confidence counts the same as damage to systems: one is an emergency that has been running since 2018, and the other is a 2021 "significant incident" power. So when officials keep saying confidence has been damaged, they are describing, on the record, the kind of harm those powers are built to answer.

- **Goal:** confidence as something to protect or rebuild.
- **Trigger:** legal text where harm to confidence helps switch on a power.
- **Unlocks:** what a trigger turns on.
- **Checked:** someone testing the claims.

Rows are in date order. Each one quotes the source's exact words.

| Short label | Use | Date | Source | Exact words | Mark |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Monitors build trust** | Goal | After 2013 (undated) | DOJ fact sheet on enforcement after *Shelby County* | election monitoring helps "deter wrongdoing, defuse tension, promote compliance with the law and bolster public confidence in the electoral process" ([timeline](../timeline/?q=bolster+public+confidence)) | D |
| **The standing emergency** | Trigger | Sept 12, 2018 | Executive Order 13848, §8(f) | "foreign interference" includes acts with the purpose or effect of "undermining public confidence in election processes" ([timeline](../timeline/?q=13848+sec.+8(f))) | D |
| **"Impossible to disprove"** | Checked | Jan 15, 2020 | Intelligence community memo on 2020 election infrastructure | adversaries could "link an exaggerated claim ... to actual operations," and such claims "would be difficult ... or impossible" to disprove ([timeline](../timeline/?q=nicm+2020-003)) | D |
| **No evidence** | Checked | Mar 16, 2021 | DOJ and DHS findings on 2020 (the EO 13848 report) | "no evidence that any foreign government-affiliated actor prevented voting, changed votes ... or otherwise compromised the integrity of voter registration information" ([timeline](../timeline/?q=1(b)+report+key+findings)) | D |
| **The 2021 switch** | Trigger | Nov 15, 2021 | Cyber Response and Recovery Act, 6 U.S.C. 677a(8) | a "significant incident" is one that causes, or "is likely to result" in, demonstrable harm to "the public confidence, civil liberties, or public health and safety of the people of the United States" ([timeline](../timeline/?q=677a(8))) | D |
| **What the switch unlocks** | Unlocks | Dec 22, 2023 | 10 U.S.C. 12304(c), added by the FY2024 defense bill | after a declared significant incident, the Defense Secretary may order reserve units to active duty for up to 365 days without their consent ([timeline](../timeline/?q=12304(c)+covered+incident)) | D |
| **Emergency renewed** | Trigger | Aug 31, 2026 | Presidential notice continuing the EO 13848 emergency | "Continuation of the National Emergency With Respect to Foreign Interference in or Undermining Public Confidence in United States Elections" ([timeline](../timeline/?q=13848+continuing)) | D |
| **"No-fail mission"** | Goal | Sept 22, 2026 | Defense Secretary Hegseth's memo to the intelligence agencies | "mobilize every authorized asset, capability, and partnership under your command to defend our election infrastructure from foreign malign influence"; "a no-fail mission" ([timeline](../timeline/?q=no-fail+mission)) | D |
| **"Restore confidence"** | Goal | Nov 2, 2026 | DOJ release on 63 people charged | DOJ "will continue investigating and prosecuting voter fraud to restore confidence in our electoral system" ([timeline](../timeline/?q=restore+confidence)) | D |
| **The report card** | Trigger (pending) | Due about Dec 18, 2026 | EO 13848 §1(a) | the intelligence assessment on foreign interference is due "Not later than 45 days after the conclusion of" the election; the DOJ/DHS report follows about Feb 1, 2027 ([board](../tripwires/)) | D |

**What it shows (our read, I):** "public confidence" is a fine goal and a loaded trigger at the same time. Under the 2018 emergency, which is still in effect, foreign acts that undermine confidence count as interference. Under the 2021 law, harm to confidence can help justify a "significant incident," which can lead to reserve call-ups. The limits matter: the 2021 law still needs an actual cyber incident on an information system, and the 2018 order needs a foreign actor. Rhetoric alone triggers neither. But a claimed foreign hack plus months of "confidence" talk is the shortest path to both, which is why the board watches row 1 and row 3 together.

## #4 · "Fraud," "knowingly," "aiders and abettors": who the crime is aimed at {#fraud-or-crime}

Voting by noncitizens is a real federal crime, and the element that makes it one is that the person votes *knowingly*. This year that word has been on the move: from the voter, to the election official who counts the ballot, to the group that registered the voter. Watch who each threat is aimed at.

- **At voters:** warnings and charges aimed at people who vote.
- **At officials:** criminal exposure for the people who run and count the election.
- **At organizers:** "fraud" and "terrorism" framing for groups that register voters or protest.
- **Checked:** a court or the facts pushing back.

## #5 · "Access to everything": the authority breadcrumbs {#access-to-everything}

This is the paper trail of permission. Executive orders, bills and memos keep telling agencies and task forces to reach for every record, every database and every asset. Some of these grants stay inside the law; the trail is how you see when the reach gets bigger than the law behind it. Where a document writes in a limit, that's here too, right next to the power it's meant to restrain.

- **Grant:** a text that hands out access to records or data.
- **Mobilize:** an order to use every asset or act on a mandate.
- **Limit:** a written restraint on that access.
- **Checked:** a court testing the reach.

## #6 · "One record per person": the data breadcrumbs {#one-record-per-person}

This is the technology side: building one consolidated record per person out of many systems, then enriching it with commercial data. The government describes this as immigration and fraud work; from a voter's side, it's the machinery that produced the "noncitizen" lists. - **Monitor:** keeping watch on the merged records.
- **Limit:** a written restraint or denial.

## How the trail works

**Tiers**
- **A · Authority:** statute, bill, executive order, memo, directive, OLC opinion, agency plan.
- **B · Procurement and notices:** J&A / LSJ / SSJ / JEFO, SOW or PWS, RFI, solicitation, SORN, PIA, Federal Register rule.
- **C · Reports and filings:** court filings and declarations, press releases, official statements, think-tank or advocacy papers.

**Marks:** D primary text read · R reporting · I inference · U unresolved · K general knowledge, verify.

**What an entry is.** One phrase family. Each row in the chain gives the exact quoted words, document, date, tier and mark. Chains are ordered by document date, not by who wrote what. Order of appearance is a lead, not proof that one document drove another.

**Strength of a match** (rate every entry)
- **S1 exact** · identical wording of 4+ words, or 2–3 distinctive words.
- **S2 near** · same distinctive words, different order or inflection.
- **S3 concept** · same idea, different words (e.g., "golden record" vs "one consolidated record"). Useful for mapping, weakest as provenance.

**Genericity** (rate every entry)
- **G1 boilerplate** · acquisition or legal template language ("logical follow-on," "sole source," "interest of economy and efficiency"). Excluded from the ledger unless it appears in a tier-A text.
- **G2 field-common** · common in data, IT or security writing ("seamless," "unified platform," "single source of truth"). Track, but do not read as signal alone.
- **G3 distinctive** · unusual in that field, or tied to a particular advocacy or legal theory ("undiluted," "diluting the votes of citizens"). Highest value.

**Competing explanations (check for every entry)**
- H1 shared industry vocabulary (MDM, NIEM, vendor marketing).
- H2 common template or the same drafter or counsel office.
- H3 vendor-supplied capability text copied into a government justification (this one matters most for tier B).
- H4 borrowed from an earlier public source (advocacy paper, prior EO, think-tank model).
- H5 deliberate carry-through so the paper trail "fits."
No entry below settles any of these. Vendors stay neutral infrastructure: an overlap in a procurement document is not evidence that a company knew of or took part in anything.

**Log rule.** When a new document arrives, search 2–3 of its distinctive phrases in quotes against this file, the research corpus. Add a row, or add a new entry. Record "no earlier hit found" when that is the result. Quote verbatim from the source text or do not quote.

**How to read one.** Ask four questions of any promise: Which agency does it name, and which does it leave out? Which place (polling place, or the drop box, election office and counting center)? Which time (Election Day, or the weeks of counting through Jan 3)? Is it "will not," or only "no plans"?
